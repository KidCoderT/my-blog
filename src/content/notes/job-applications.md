## Resume

Your resume is one of the **first contact points** you will have with a potential employer.

- **1 to 2 pages** long
- a **record of your past achievements**
- a **marketing tool** that you use to communicate your unique value

Steps to build a resume:

1. Identify the required skills & competencies (from the job description)
2. Pick your relevant experiences and skills
3. Craft impactful resume points with **CAR statements**
4. Format

### 1. Read the job description

**Every resume is personalised.** Tailor it for each role: highlight the relevant responsibilities and experiences, and put the most relevant stuff first.

Figure out what **academic qualifications, personal qualities, skills and competencies** the recruiter is looking for.

> Ask yourself: do you actually know the basic skills and competencies this role needs?

**In the resume, make it blatantly obvious I have the necessary skills.**

#### Skills vs competencies

- **Skill**: usually a single ability or talent. You acquire, cultivate and hone it through experience, formal education and practice.
- **Core competency**: a group of related strengths, abilities or sets of skills. Behaviours, traits, talents and knowledge you use to get results in a professional situation.

E.g.

- **Networking** = active listening + communication + interpersonal skills + rapport building
- **Leadership** = decision making + influencing + visioning + adaptability

#### Explicit vs implied skills

In a job posting, find the implied skills the job is asking for:

- `Conduct research on various platforms to identify job opportunities` → research skills
- `Draft job adverts for approval and posting them on different platforms` → writing, design and language skills

#### Hard vs soft skills

And in the same way, identify the hard and soft skills:

|          | What it is                                                                 | How you prove it                                     |
| -------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| **Hard** | Technical skills necessary to perform a particular task or job             | Certificates & degree                                |
| **Soft** | Non-technical skills that can be broadly applied to other jobs/industries  | Life experiences e.g. CCAs, projects, volunteering   |

#### Let AI break down the JD

```
Analyse the following job description and help me identify
the skills, qualities, and experience the employer is looking
for. Explain the main requirements in simple language,
point out the most important resume keywords,
and identify both the skills that are directly mentioned and those that are implied
from the job description.
For any implied skills, explain how and why you inferred that these
are required skills. Explain which are skills that a candidate should emphasise
on their resume.
```

> Always validate and verify the AI's response: read what the AI says, then read the job description yourself.


### 2. Pick your experiences

| Experience                                  | What it shows                                                                                                                                       |
| ------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Work experience**                         | Internships and any real work you've done                                                                                                           |
| **Research / projects**                     | Your **understanding of theoretical concepts** and **application of technical / hard skills**. Personal and school projects both count             |
| **Global programmes / overseas**            | **Cultural awareness, adaptability** and a willingness to embrace new experiences                                                                   |
| **CCAs**                                    | **Your interests**, and how you've **applied soft skills and competencies** like collaboration, communication and leadership                        |
| **Volunteering**                            | **Empathy, compassion** and a willingness to give back to the community                                                                             |
| **Courses / certifications**                | Boosts an employer's **confidence in your ability** and your perceived value. Also shows a **love for learning** and a willingness to upskill        |

Use your experiences to demonstrate how you meet the requirements in the job description. Be _selective_ and showcase the _most relevant_ and _specific_ ones.

A good idea is to keep a record of all your experiences on a **master sheet**, so you can pick the relevant ones when customising your resume for a specific job description. And be intentional about building up relevant experiences for your career goals.

And in the resume **Use the language of the job description, for the job application!**

### 3. Write points with CAR

A resume is not a laundry list of records, skills and experiences. It's a **branding tool**. It tells the **story of who you are** and **what value you bring** to the company. It's not just a factual doc, it's there to _persuade_ the recruiter you fit the role.

The formula:

**Strong verb → how you did it → the result**

#### C: Competency

Start your bullet point with an impactful competency **verb** to highlight your exact contribution and value-add to a project or team.

- ✅ Optimised, Boosted, Organised, Spearheaded, Designed, Developed, Led, Pitched
- ❌ Carried out, Participated, Helped out, Learnt

> In general, verbs like "supported" and "assisted" communicate a backseat role or a minor contribution. It creates the impression that you were only a sidekick to someone else.

Pick verbs that match **the role** and **your personal brand / USP**.

#### A: Action

Find the action words in the job posting itself and reuse them. But don't stay generic, get specific!

Apart from using verbs relevant to the JD, you can also highlight a strength by mentioning verbs from your dominant **RIASEC** trait(s) multiple times. Quick reminder of the traits:

- **Realistic**: practical, hands-on, tools & equipment
- **Investigative**: precise, scientific, facts, ideas, theories
- **Artistic**: intuitive, expressive, creative, innovative
- **Social**: helping others, outgoing, working with people
- **Enterprising**: persuade, lead, sell, influence
- **Conventional**: well-organised, detail-oriented, accurate, structured

| 🟦 Realistic | 🟨 Investigative | 🟥 Artistic     | 🔵 Social     | 🟧 Enterprising | ⬜ Conventional |
| ----------- | --------------- | -------------- | ------------ | -------------- | -------------- |
| Assembled   | Analysed        | Designed       | Advised      | Led            | Organised      |
| Built       | Researched      | Created        | Taught       | Negotiated     | Processed      |
| Installed   | Diagnosed       | Illustrated    | Facilitated  | Pitched        | Documented     |
| Operated    | Evaluated       | Composed       | Mentored     | Persuaded      | Structured     |
| Engineered  | Modelled        | Conceptualised | Tutored      | Spearheaded    | Scheduled      |
| Repaired    | Investigated    | Drafted        | Coached      | Promoted       | Audited        |
| Maintained  | Synthesised     | Scripted       | Counselled   | Influenced     | Filed          |
| Calibrated  | Interpreted     | Edited         | Educated     | Mobilised      | Systematised   |
| Constructed | Computed        | Innovated      | Mediated     | Initiated      | Administered   |
| Developed   | Formulated      | Curated        | Collaborated | Delivered      | Tracked        |

After the verb, **describe the role you played** and **explain how you fulfilled your responsibilities**.

This is also your chance to **mention the software or machinery** you used to get the job done, especially for technical roles. But don't fill it with technical jargon: non-technical HR recruiters won't understand your points.

E.g.

- Built a dynamic website using **Python** with **RESTful** API integration for real-time data retrieval and user interaction.
- Integrated multiple third-party services by consuming and processing **JSON**-based API responses, streamlining workflows and improving feature reliability.
- Wrote complex **SQL** queries to extract, filter, and analyse large datasets (**more than 1 million rows**) to support business decisions with accurate and actionable insights.

#### R: Result or Reason

Finally, **quantify your achievements**. But if these happen, do this:

| Problem                         | Do this                                                                                                     |
| ------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Can't be quantified / shared    | Quantify your actions and show the objectives of your task, e.g. "Collaborated with X stakeholders"         |
| Product not launched yet        | State the potential impact and target instead                                                               |
| Can't remember the number       | Give an approximation, a plausible range                                                                    |
| No quantifiable outcome at all  | A qualitative outcome (_...improving cross-departmental relationships_) or the rationale of the project (_...to improve organisation culture and engagement_) |

It is important to quantify your results, but you should **NOT** fabricate results!!!

So the idea: **Start with a strong action word → summarise how you carried out the task → quantify the result achieved**

#### Before → After

**Part-Time Tutor → Part-Time Math Tutor**

- ❌ Taught English and Math lessons
- ✅ **Conducted** English and Math lessons for five Primary 6 students and improved their grades from Band 3 to Band 1
  - Scope (five P6 students) + a hard result (Band 3 → Band 1).
- ❌ Responsible for marking assessment papers / Assisted with students' Math questions
- ✅ **Incorporated** fun experiments and animations in lessons to trigger interest and deepen students' understanding of core Math concepts
  - "Responsible for" and "assisted" are passive. This shows _your_ idea and _why_ it mattered.
- ❌ Answered parents' queries
- ✅ **Provided** timely feedback through weekly emails to parents so they could be updated on their child's progress
  - Reacting → being proactive, with a how (weekly emails) and a reason.

**Project Lead**

- ❌ Looked for sponsors for TEDx Talk event
- ✅ **Prospected** 10 sponsor companies from Finance and Technology industries, and raised the highest amount across the past 3 years of TEDx Talks
  - Numbers for scope (10 companies, 2 industries) and a result compared against past years.
- ❌ Led a team to organise programmes for welfare organisation
- ✅ **Led** team of 5 members to organise a carnival that attracted a breakthrough attendance of 300 volunteers and beneficiaries of various ages and disabilities
  - Same verb, but now you know the team size, the event and the turnout.
- ❌ Completed field surveys for projects
- ✅ **Reviewed** field survey methodology and streamlined the data collection process for various biodiversity projects, improving team efficiency and productivity by 30%
  - "Completed" is just doing the task. "Reviewed + streamlined" shows you improved it, and by how much.

#### What to avoid

1. **Describing the task → showcase your skills!**\
   Avoid simply listing your duties, it won't differentiate you from other candidates. Focus on the **competencies, skills and results** you showed while doing them.
2. **Describing what you learnt → show evidence of your competencies!**\
   Avoid focusing on what you gained. Employers want to know how you will **contribute**.
   - ❌ `Honed strong communication skills`
   - ✅ `Communicated with clients, consultants and vendors on project requirements and successfully ensured that project milestones were met`
3. **Vague or general statements → focus on details and scope!**\
   Employers may not understand the impact and depth of your work. Include details and numbers!

### 4. Resume format

#### Chronological vs functional

- **Reverse chronological**: recommended for fresh graduates. It highlights your most current work experiences and achievements first.
- **Functional**: if your most relevant skills aren't in your most current experiences, especially when applying for a more technical role.

#### Sections (choose based on the skills & competencies from the JD)

1. **Name, contact details, LinkedIn, GitHub URL** (for computing students). Avoid hyperlinks, they can mess up the ATS.
2. **Education**: for fresh grads and entry-level jobs, it's good to start with this.
3. **Skills**: for functional resumes and technical roles, list the technical tools, platforms and frameworks you know. **Avoid soft skills here**, they aren't measurable and shouldn't be self-assessed.
4. **Internship / Work Experience**: exclusively internships and work experience.
5. **Projects**: personal or school-based. Especially important for technical or research roles. In a functional resume, Experience and Projects can swap depending on relevance to the JD.
6. **CCAs / Leadership / Volunteering**: to showcase relevant soft skills and competencies.
7. **Certificates & Other Qualifications**: growth mindset, love for learning, deepening of skills.
8. **Awards**: excellence in a field or a particular strength (e.g. Eagles Award for leadership, Math Olympiad medals).

#### Example: Education + Skills

```
National University of Singapore
- Bachelor of Computing in Computer Science
- GPA: 4.6 / 5 (First Class Honours)
- Relevant coursework: Programming Methodology, Introduction to Operating Systems,
  Introduction to Information Security, Programming Languages

Skills
- Programming Languages: Python, C++, HTML, JavaScript, C#
- Software Frameworks: React, .NET Framework
- Database Systems: PostgreSQL
- Software: MS Excel
```

- Write the course **titles, not just the codes**, so recruiters understand what you learnt. Only the ones relevant to the role.
- For technical roles, put your skills section at the top and organise it into **neat categories**. Recruiters take **less than 10 seconds** to scan a resume.
- Soft skills are shown through your choice of verbs and experiences, not self-evaluated. In short: **soft skills are shown, not told.**
- Be sure to clarify terms that only insiders of an organisation or community would know. Don't assume recruiters know what they mean or will find out.

#### ATS-friendly formatting

| ✅ Do                                                                           | ❌ Don't                                                          |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1 page ideally, 2 max                                                          | Multiple columns or tables                                       |
| Single column layout, basic bullet points                                      | Images or profile pics (not needed in Singapore unless asked)    |
| Simple fonts: Arial, Verdana, Calibri, Garamond, Helvetica, Times New Roman    | Info in headers / footers (the ATS can drop it completely)       |
| Size 11–12 for text, 14–16 for headings                                        | Creative headings like "Where I've Made an Impact"               |
| Black text on white, balance of text and space                                 | Creative / design elements                                       |
| 0.5 to 1 inch margins all around                                               | Leaving out months in dates                                      |
| Standard headings: Education, Work Experience, CCAs, Skills                    | Hyperlinks                                                       |
| Dates as MMM YYYY (e.g. Jul 2026), consistent throughout                       |                                                                  |
| Proofread: spelling, grammar, punctuation, formatting (mistakes look careless) |                                                                  |
| Save as **PDF**. Applying by email? Add a brief note in the body stating your purpose |                                                           |

## Cover Letter

A cover letter is a well-written **1-page letter** that _complements_ your resume. It lets you showcase your **personality**, give **context** to your accomplishments, and show your suitability for the role in a way a resume can't. It's a _persuasive piece of writing_.

A cover letter is really just 3 parts:

- Hi to the recruiter
- Why you want the job
- Your experiences

(+ a small thank you, or explaining something like a gap in your resume, and your signature.)

> The first thing I look for in a cover letter is whether it is addressed to the right person.\
> **MAKE SURE TO ADDRESS IT RIGHT.**

What makes a candidate stand out (Jobscan, 2025 State of the Job Search):

| What they did                                          | %               |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | --------------- |
| Showed measurable achievements on their resume         | 58.2%           |
| Tailored their resume to the job description           | 55.3%           |
| **Wrote a personalised cover letter that highlights their value** | **54%** |
| LinkedIn profile matches their application             | 46.8%           |
| Networked to build relationships in target companies   | 44.7%           |
| Followed up professionally after applying              | 24.4%           |

### Resume vs cover letter

| Resume                                          | Cover letter                                                                   |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Summary of your experiences and qualifications  | Explains **2–3 selected** qualities or skills                                  |
| Bullet points, so recruiters can skim           | Well-elaborated paragraphs that also show off your communication skills        |
| Factual and objective: who, what, when          | More subjective: your personality and the **why** behind your application      |
| No motivation for joining the organisation      | **Must** include why you want to join, so research what they do                |

Cover letters are especially useful when you **don't have much experience**, or the role is **very different from your course of study**. Use it to explain **your motivation** (why this organisation and role), emphasise relevant **hard / soft skills**, and highlight **experiences** that give you an edge (projects, interests, CCAs, internships, volunteering...).

> If someone says no cover letter... don't throw the cover letter down their throat... just give them what they want.\
> If they say optional, then go above and beyond and try your best.

### Do your research

Same as the resume: it has to be **tailored**. Your message needs to be **specific** and **compelling** enough to grab the employer's attention.

1. **Job description**: go over it and pick the experiences that best match their posting.
2. **Organisation**: LOOK THROUGH THE ORGANISATION. Their values, mission, goals, milestones, news and reputation, and talk about them. Explain why you applied to them and how you as a person fit their company.
3. **Industry trends**: look up the industry, link that info to the company, and show how you can help. It shows an eye for detail.
   - Factiva and Euromonitor Passport via the [NUS Libraries' portal](https://nus.edu.sg/nuslibraries/databases-search)
   - SkillsFuture's [Career Resources](https://www.myskillsfuture.gov.sg/content/portal/en/career-resources/career-resources.html)
   - Market research providers (e.g. [PwC's market research and insights](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en))
   - [Google News](https://news.google.com/) & [Google Trends](https://trends.google.com/)
4. **Go beyond the letterhead**: reach out to the company's people on LinkedIn or at their events. Ask seniors about the company and what they look for in a candidate. Use all of this when deciding what goes in your letter.

### Structure

1. **Salutation**: always address it to a _named contact_. Search LinkedIn, the company website or your network for the hiring manager's name and position. Can't find one? "Dear Hiring Manager".
2. **Application title**: the role you're applying for.
3. **Section 1**: why this company and role.
4. **Section 2**: why me? Your experience and accomplishments, expanding on the resume.
5. **Section 3**: final call to action. Reaffirm why you want to join, thank the employer for considering your application, include your contact details and close!

**The opening:** applying to a creative role or industry? A dynamic opening with a personal touch can work. Traditional sectors? A strong and simple opening will do.

**Don't duplicate your resume.** Refer to it and **expand on it**. Include a **brief but illustrative anecdote**, and explain directly how your skills and experience fit the role and set you apart.

### Checklist

- **Length**: no more than 1 page.
- **Font**: simple and professional (e.g. Arial, Verdana), size 11–12.
- **Presentation**: proofread for spelling, grammar, punctuation and consistent formatting. Make sure it passes the ATS too!
- **Email etiquette**: applying by email? Attach the cover letter (and resume) as PDFs and add a brief note in the body stating your purpose.

### Using AI

**What AI can help with:**

- **Brainstorming**: give it the company, the JD and your resume, and ask which experiences are most relevant to include.
- **Editing**: proofreading, refining the tone to match the company and audience.
- **Checking relevancy**: did you include the crucial JD keywords? Or ask it to act as the hiring manager and critique your letter against the JD.

**What you need to own:**

- **Accuracy**: AI hallucinates. It can make up experiences and traits you don't have. Everything in the final cut must be backed by real experiences.
- **Your story**: your past, your future ambition, your motivation, and how it all connects. Make sure the draft is a true reflection of it.
- **Your personal voice**: pure AI cover letters all sound the same, and recruiters can tell! Edit it into your own voice and style.

### Common mistakes

**1. Letting self-doubt in.** Phrases like "I have not acquired..." make you seem weak. Keep the tone **upbeat and positive**.

- ❌ "Although I have no / do not have much experience in...", "Although I am new to..."
- ✅ "This is an exciting challenge that I would love to work on." / "Working with a start-up involves initiative and adaptability, and these skills that I have developed will serve me well in the fast-paced and ever-changing finance industry as well."

**2. Being too self-focused.** Don't only talk about your own growth ("...a catalyst for **my** career"). Show you care about the company and how you can help it and its users grow. Use more "you" and "your" sentences.

- ❌ "I am interested in..." (multiple times), "I believe this is a great opportunity for me to learn and contribute to your top-notch company which I strongly feel has the appropriate resources and mentorship to groom fresh graduates like myself."
- ✅ "Your company has recently announced a goal to reduce emissions by 25%...", "Your organisational values of excellence and quality..."

**3. Being too general.** Generic reasons show a lack of research, or that you don't understand the company well. Be specific.

- ❌ "It would be my privilege to start a career here...", "I am interested to join you as you are one of the top pharmaceutical companies..."
- ✅ "Company X has recently announced a strategic collaboration with Corporation Y to accelerate digital cross-border trade, serving the company's mission to democratise access to cross-border markets..."

**4. Making claims without proof.** Saying "I believe in my ability to think critically" is not enough. Every skill needs an example that demonstrates it, with results and outcomes.

- ❌ "I strongly believe I have the necessary experience and aptitude to thrive and contribute in a meaningful way to your firm."
- ✅ "I have developed strong project management and enhanced my marketing skills through the campaigns I took part in during my CCAs and part-time job. Two out of three marketing campaigns that I supported exceeded the target goals by 50–100%, in addition to a 20% increase in online engagement and followers on Facebook and Instagram."

**5. Sounding passive.** "I was tasked with..." makes you seem passive. Show that you took ownership of the work, using dynamic action words.

- ❌ "I was tasked / assigned to...", "I was told / asked to...", "I was entrusted..."
- ✅ "Having piloted an initiative to...", "Organising a marketing campaign..."

## LinkedIn

> The sad thing..... It's unavoidable.\
> This world works on attention.\
> It doesn't give a shit how skilled you are unless you tell it to others.\
> And LinkedIn is what people use as an accountability tool.\
> Develop it well and you can improve your odds.

Why bother:

- **Your digital resume.** It helps you articulate your personal brand (goals, values, interests) even before you enter the workforce.
- **Recruiters search on it** for interns, part-timers and student ambassadors. Having a profile puts you on their radar. You can also browse for applications and more through it.
- **Networking.** Many alumni and industry professionals are open to connecting with students. Learn from their career paths, read their content, and ask for advice.
- **The feed.** Articles, events, career advice and news in your areas of interest. Follow professionals, companies, universities and student clubs to keep up.

**One thing about LinkedIn when you start... the same thing about life as a whole. Be consistent.**

Share updates when you:

- join a club
- complete a course
- attend a talk
- achieve something meaningful
- learn something from a recent project or event

Or leave thoughtful comments on other people's posts. It keeps your profile dynamic and engaging.

Your LinkedIn profile is not something you need to "perfect" right away. It's a **living document** that evolves with your experiences and aspirations. Use it to get a head start on building a professional presence, expanding your network and discovering opportunities.

- Your resume and cover letter still matter for the job.
- LinkedIn enhances them, and can even put your application in front of more people. (In the Jobscan survey above, a LinkedIn that matches your application scored 46.8% for making a candidate stand out.)

## Interviews

See.... an interviewer wants to find their applicant as soon as possible.\
They chose you because they believe you might fit. So stop thinking they are plotting a court trial to scrutinise you for something.....

An interview isn't an interrogation, it's a **two-way conversation**. They assess you, and you find out more about the role, the employer and the industry, and whether it's the right opportunity for **you**.

### Types of interviews

1. **Phone**: usually a screening interview to narrow the talent pool. Salary expectations, confirming skills, or starting an assessment.
   - Pay attention to any call after you apply. It might be the first interview! Not in a good place to talk? Thank the caller, get their number, and arrange to call back.
   - _Every touchpoint is part of the evaluation._ Stay professional.
2. **Digital**: Google Meet.... that's it.
3. **In person**
   - **One-on-one**: the most common. Conversational; the interviewer drives at the start, you ask questions at the end.
   - **Panel**: multiple people at once, usually your future supervisor + staff from other departments, for a more moderated assessment.
   - **Group**: multiple candidates for the same role in one room. You may get info about the company and role first, then answer questions or do group exercises.

   > There can be many rounds...

4. **Networking sessions**: lunch, dinner or coffee outside the office. They're assessing how you behave in a social setting and how comfortable you are with corporate people. **It's still an interview**, act professionally.
5. **Technical**: for software roles. Code in a language to show your competency: over the phone, an online challenge, or on a whiteboard.

#### Video interviews (one-way)

Just be ready beforehand.

Platforms like **HireVue** and **Truffle** let recruiters run asynchronous interviews: you record answers to pre-set questions, with no one on the other end. Their algorithms analyse facial expressions, eye & body movement, clothes and tone of voice to predict performance.

- Prepare like it's face-to-face
- Check your connection
- Dress appropriately
- Look at the **camera lens**, not the screen
- Speak clearly, avoid stutters and long pauses
- Watch your facial expressions & body language
- Rehearse: record yourself and watch it back

#### Tests & assessments

> If the interviewer gives you some game or task... do it ASAP and with focus, even if it's something small. It might be a cognitive test.

| Test                                | What it checks                                                             |
| ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Numerical reasoning**             | O-level maths, basic data-driven analysis and conclusions                  |
| **Verbal reasoning**                | Comprehension, logic / inference / deduction, analytical skills, critical thinking |
| **Situational judgement & in-tray** | Strategic thinking, problem solving, company-specific skills / values (e.g. "Customer First", "Innovation") |
| **Psychometrics**                   | Working style, motivations, values, traits (drive, diligence, risk taking), cognitive ability |
| **Role plays / simulations**        | Emotional intelligence, teamwork, communication                            |

### First impressions

ALWAYS REMEMBER

> **"You never get a second chance to make a first impression."**\
> — Will Rogers

The important factors:

| #   | Factor                    | Rated important by |
| --- | ------------------------- | ------------------ |
| 1   | Punctuality               | 96%                |
| 2   | Interview preparation     | 93%                |
| 3   | Eye contact               | 82%                |
| 4   | Attire / grooming         | 73%                |
| 5   | Small talk / conversation | 60%                |
| 6   | Handshake                 | 55%                |

**Punctuality.** On time (or early) = respect. Late = the exact opposite, no matter how qualified you are. Aim for **at least 10 min early** in person. Virtual too: it gives you time to test your audio and video.

**Preparation.** That's steps 1–4 below.

**Eye contact.** Conveys interest, sincerity and confidence. Looking down, looking around or avoiding eye contact signals nervousness and a lack of trust.

**Attire / grooming.** Sharp and professional = prepared and serious. They need to be able to picture you in the role. Still matters on video: on a light background, wear a darker, contrasting top. Safe colours: white, greys, maroon, navy, neutrals, light blue, light pink.

**Small talk.** If they ask how you got here or where you live, they're testing your small talk. It shows you can build rapport beyond the script. Good starters:

1. Thank them for the opportunity and their time.
2. Reiterate your enthusiasm for the role.
3. Briefly share your research on the company or role, and that you look forward to learning more.

**Handshake.** Firm, not limp, but not crushing. Follow their lead: fist bump or no hand? Do the same, and smile: "Hello, I'm XXX, it's great to meet you."

### 1. Organise yourself

- Put the date and time in your calendar and set reminders.
- Plan to arrive **15–30 min early** for delays.
- Check the address and plan your route, plus a backup route or transport.
- If it's a big building... be sure you know the building beforehand.
- Bring what HR asks for (resume, cover letter, testimonials, transcripts), arranged neatly in a file so you can pull them out easily.
- Dress professionally. Don't assume what the employees wear is what you should wear. **Over-dressed beats under-dressed.** Unsure? Ask HR.
- With a suit, carry a professional file / folio, or a clutch / satchel if you need a bag. No backpacks or duffels.
- Phone out of sight and on airplane mode.

### 2. What the interviewer wants

| They want to know | Meaning                            |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| **Potential**     | Learning agility, drive, EQ        |
| **Culture fit**   | Personality, values, ethics        |
| **Commitment**    | Interest and passion               |
| **Ability**       | Skills and competencies            |

### 3. Research

Look into 3 parts:

**Industry**

- What's the value chain, and what role does the company play in it?
- What are the challenges, and the opportunities for growth?
- Who are the competitors and the other players in the ecosystem?

**Organisation**

- What products / services does it offer?
- What's its business model (how does it make money)?
- What are its mission and objectives?
- Any recent developments (challenges, new launches)?

**People**

- What types of people and skills do they need?
- What's the culture, and what do employees like about working there?
- Which team will I be joining, and who are the managers / interviewers?

Where to look:

- **LinkedIn**: the company, the industry, and seniors / alumni from your faculty working there. Also **your interviewers**: don't know who they are? Politely ask HR for their names and roles. Their background, position and any common ground with you is great for small talk, and knowing who they are lowers the psychological barrier so you warm up faster.
- **Latest news**: be up to date, and see how the company compares to others in the industry.
- **Glassdoor**: inside details you won't find on the company website: salaries, duties, reviews, the hiring process. But nothing is verified, so take it with a pinch of salt.
- **NUS Libraries e-resources - USE IT.** Factiva by Dow Jones is available to all NUS students.

### 4. Prepare for the questions

#### Question types

| Type                        | What they're testing                                            | Example                                                    |
| --------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Personal narrative**      | Who you are, your values, strengths and value proposition       | "Tell me about yourself"                                   |
| **Industry awareness**      | Whether you researched the industry, company and role           | "What are the challenges facing the industry?"             |
| **Behavioural**             | How you acted in the past, to predict how you'll act in future  | "Tell me about a time you resolved a conflict in a team"   |
| **Hypothetical**            | Your "operating system": values, judgement, decision making     | "If you witness unethical behaviour, what would you do?"   |
| **Skill-based / technical** | Domain knowledge and skills                                     | "List some best practices for data cleaning"               |
| **Curveball**               | Staying composed and thinking on your feet under stress         | "How many traffic lights are there in Singapore?"          |

Interviewers usually **start with personal narrative** questions. For technical roles (programmer, engineer, research assistant), expect an actual task: a coding problem, a policy recommendation, or an engineering / research problem.

#### Answer frameworks

- **Past → Present → Future**: start with where you are now, then the past that led you here, then what you hope to do next. It tells a story and connects your past to your future.
- **3-point technique**: structure the answer as 3 points. "Firstly... Secondly... Thirdly..." Concise and memorable.
- **S.T.A.R.**: for real examples or hypothetical scenarios.
  - **S**ituation
  - **T**ask
  - **A**ction
  - **R**esult and/or **Reflection**: R isn't just the outcome, it's also what you reflected on and learnt.

#### Going deep on the common questions

Really, let's go deep into the questions.

**"Tell me about yourself"**

- _They're really asking_: can you describe yourself succinctly, communicate your strengths and interests (your personal brand), and show your relevance to the company and role?
- ❌ Too long or too short, irrelevant details. You don't need to go from birth to present in order......
- ✅ **Present** (who I am) → **past** (what I've done) → **future** (what I want).

**"What are your strengths?"**

- ✅ Use the 3-point technique. Capitalise on your top 3 skills, with detail.

**"Why should we hire you?"**

- ❌ The student mindset: I want to grow, get experience, I'm passionate and all that.
- ✅ The employee mindset: the industry is growing and you want to contribute. You have the skills and the ability to contribute to the company.

**"What do you know about our company?"**

- _They're really asking_: really think... this is a proper research and interest check. Do you know the job scope, their products and services, strategies, and latest concerns and developments?
- ❌ Regurgitating their website, or easy facts that show no in-depth knowledge or insight.

**"Tell us about a time you had to resolve conflict in a team"**

- _They're really asking_: your future behaviour based on past experience, and whether you can use a real experience to show a skill and what you learnt.
- ❌ No self-reflection, focusing too much on the negative, too little or irrelevant detail.
- ✅ Use **S.T.A.R.** Show the process and how it led to a positive outcome or learning points.

**"What would you do if you witness unethical behaviour at work?"**

- _They're really asking_: your values and principles, your knowledge of the role, your thought process, and whether you can weigh cause and effect, pros and cons.
- ❌ Linear logic with only one course of action, or not saying how your values guide you.
- ✅ Answer with S.T.A.R. again... and give your reasoning.

**Curveballs**

- _They're really asking_: can you stay composed, think on your feet, reason logically (sometimes with numbers) from assumptions, and be creative?
- ❌ Just giving a specific answer without explaining your thought process.
- ✅ There are no right or wrong answers. Share your thought process, your assumptions and why you made them.
- ✅ You can always ask more about things though. Clarifying questions are fine!

### 5. The closing: your questions

At the end, they will **always** ask if you have any questions. Always prepare a few.

- No questions can show you're not very interested.
- Good questions show you're well prepared and genuinely interested in joining.
- It also makes you more memorable.

A good set:

**The role**

- What projects would I be working on in this role?
- What are your expectations of this role in the first 1–2 months?
- How will you measure the success of the person in this position?
- Looking at people who have filled this role before, what differentiated the most successful ones from the rest?

**The team & business**

- What are some of the pain points your clients need help with?
- What are the biggest opportunities and challenges facing the department now?

**Culture & growth**

- What do you like best about working here?
- How would you describe the work culture?
- How does the organisation support employees' professional development and career growth?
- What is one thing you would change about the company if you could?

Use AI to brainstorm:

- generate likely interview questions
- simulate mock interviews
- come up with insightful questions to ask

### During & after

- **Let the interviewer lead.** Don't interrupt, be ready for small talk, and listen actively. Paraphrase or clarify a question if you need time to collect your thoughts.
- **Don't bring up salary or benefits** unless they do. Salary negotiation usually comes later, with HR.
- **Send a thank-you note** after the interview. No emails? Send it to the HR manager and ask them to forward it.


## Networking

> **Good networking is just like asking for directions. It is not manipulation or trying to make use of people.**

### Why it matters

The harsh truth: about **70% of jobs are never publicly posted**. That's the **hidden job market**. Employers use it to skip the long and expensive process of open online applications.

And referrals are how you get in. Referred candidates are:

- **more likely to get an interview**
- **about 7 times more likely to get hired**
- through **a faster hiring process**

Companies even run **employee referral programs**, and the employee who referred you often gets a bonus if you're hired. Everyone wins.

In LinkedIn's _Networking Your Way In_ survey, **82% of Singaporeans** agreed networking is important for career success. **54%** got a job introduction through a casual LinkedIn conversation, and **52%** got a job because they knew someone at the company.

Networking lets you:

- ✅ **Access hidden jobs** through referrals, alongside social media, recruiting firms and other outreach
- ✅ **Explore careers** with professionals in similar or different organisations, roles and industries
- ✅ **Get trusted advice** from mentors, domain experts, industry practitioners and leaders
- ✅ **Build your brand**, so people know who you are and your unique value

**The power of relationships.** Keith Ferrazzi's big idea: the secret to getting ahead is reaching out to other people. What separates highly successful people from everyone else is how they use the power of relationships, so that everyone wins.

**Reciprocity.** Through networking you show your value, create value for others, and get value back.

**There's no point being the best coder, the best researcher, the best digital marketer, if no one knows it.**

### How to meaningfully connect

1. **Figure out who's going to be there.** RESEARCH the venue and its people in advance. How? Be of service to the host / organisers, then ask about the guest list.
2. **Pick who you want to meet.** Once you have the list, ask the organiser or host if they could introduce you.
3. **Show up with 5 packets of generosity.** 5 things you can do to be of service and make them comfortable: introduce them to people in their field, talk about something they do, share ideas, and so on.
4. **Be aggressively vulnerable.** "Do you guys mind if I just break into this conversation? Standing there by myself is making me nervous."
5. **Just be you.**

**Humility, authenticity, reciprocity → I want to help them.**

### 1. Who's in your network?

**You already have a network.** Start with your **current connections**. It's not just **who do you know**, it's **who do they know**? One person can connect you to several others.

Networking happens in everyday places:

| Who                                                   | How they network                                                    |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Undergrad working at a bar / eatery in Holland Village | Chats with customers during quiet periods to learn about companies and careers |
| PMET volunteering as a trainer at a professional body | Talks to class participants from different backgrounds              |
| Intern at a staff birthday from another department    | Finds out what they do, their interesting projects and business insights |

### 2. Growing your network

The best way to build a big network: **get involved in as many things that matter to you as possible.**

- Care about dogs? Volunteer at the SPCA, lead dog walks or a team of volunteers.
- Like to code? Contribute on GitHub or join a hackathon.
- Enjoy running? Join or start a jogging club.

Your network is made of **strong ties** and **weak ties**:

|                 | Who                                                     | What they are |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------- |
| **Strong ties** | Family, close friends, coworkers                        | **Bonds**     |
| **Weak ties**   | Acquaintances you've met in professional or personal settings | **Bridges** |

Mark Granovetter's study _The Strength of Weak Ties_ found that weak ties spread information across different groups. They have access to new info, like job openings, that your tight circle can't see. And the jobs found through weak ties came with **higher pay and satisfaction**.

But a strong tie... can introduce you to a new group!

> "...when it comes to finding out about new jobs - or, for that matter, new information, or new ideas - 'weak ties' are always more important than strong ties."\
> — Mark Granovetter, _Getting a Job: A Study of Contacts and Careers_

- **More connections isn't always better.** A huge network costs more time and effort to maintain.
- **Not all connections are equal.** Strong = family, coworkers, friends. Weak = acquaintances.
- **Weak connections expand your network.** For new jobs, info and ideas, they matter more than strong ones.

### 3. Getting over the fear

Talking to strangers, making small talk, keeping a conversation going: these are real fears, and very common, even for adults. There's also the assumption that introverts can't network.

That's a mindset thing. Carol Dweck's research: people with a **fixed mindset** believe their intelligence and talent are fixed. People with a **growth mindset** believe their abilities can be developed through dedication and hard work. Brains and talent are just the starting point. Growth mindset people tend to be more successful.

| ❌ Fixed mindset                   | ✅ Growth mindset                              |
| --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| I am not good at talking to people | I can learn and get better at engaging people |
| I can only apply for an ICT job   | I can learn to excel in any industry          |
| Dodge challenges                  | Welcome challenges                            |
| Take criticism personally         | Don't take criticism personally               |
| Threatened by others' success     | Learn from others' success                    |

### 4. The elevator pitch

A short, sharp introduction of yourself to spark interest in who you are. **20–30 seconds.** Share some interesting details, keep it memorable, and engage the person you're talking to.

| Content                | Communication           | Non-verbal               |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Educational background | Clarity                 | Appropriate attire       |
| Goals / objectives     | Projecting confidence   | Direct eye contact       |
| Skills / attributes    | Minimal fillers         | Upright posture          |
|                        | Pronunciation & grammar | Friendly demeanour, smile |

The template:

1. **Who am I?** "Hi, my name is [Name]. I'm a current [Year of study] NUS student, majoring in [Major]."
   - _e.g. Hi, my name is X. I'm a current Y3 NUS student, majoring in Business Analytics at the School of Computing._
2. **Why am I talking to you?** The challenge you want to work on. "I'm really interested in [challenge] because [reason]. I saw you had a [role] open and I'm very interested in applying, as it's a great opportunity for me to [how it links to your challenge]."
   - _e.g. I'm really interested in using data to drive business decisions, as it can create products that give real value to consumers. I saw your company has a Business Analyst role... I see it as a great opportunity to use data to unearth insights that help solve your business challenges._
3. **How do I want others to see me?** Your strength, from your RIASEC trait (see the table in the Resume section). "As someone who is [RIASEC trait], I believe I'll make a good [role] because [how that trait helps in the role]."
   - _e.g. As someone naturally curious and investigative, I believe I'll make a good Business Analyst because I'm always trying different ways to analyse data to uncover new findings._
4. **What have I done to back it up?** "For example, in my [relevant experience], I was able to [what you did]. That led to [impact]." (Basically a CAR point said out loud.)
   - _e.g. When I was interning at Y, my investigative nature helped me spot hidden trends in customer behaviour using data visualisation and predictive modelling. I found churn was driven by a previously overlooked factor, which led to a strategy change that improved retention by Z%._
5. **Call to action.** "I believe my [strengths and traits] will be especially beneficial to the [role] at your company, should I be given the chance to be part of the team."

### 5. Keep the conversation going: be curious

Just like Curiosity the rover explores Mars, **curiosity** when you network helps you **explore relationships and opportunities with people**.

Stick to **open-ended questions**. It's hard to keep a conversation going when the other person can only say yes or no.

| ❌ Closed (fizzles out)               | ✅ Open-ended (keeps going)                          |
| ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------- |
| How long have you been working here? | What made you explore a career in this?             |
| What's your role?                    | What's the day-to-day life of a manager like?       |
| Do you like your job?                | What do you enjoy about the job?                    |
| What are the working hours?          |                                                     |

The classic conversation killers start with **When? Where? Who? Which? Are? Do?**

Prompts to keep it flowing (keep it authentic and actually be interested):

- "Tell me more"
- "What happened next?"
- "How are you feeling about it?"

And ask for their **personal take**: their opinions and advice.

### 6. Happenstance: make your own luck

You probably know people who seem unusually lucky in their careers, who stumbled on something good by being in the right place at the right time.

What you don't see is that they were almost always **primed** to take those opportunities, by actions they took. **They were curious, they investigated, they connected with people.**

The theory: you can increase your chances of these lucky breaks by staying curious, deliberately putting yourself in the way of something new, and learning to recognise and grab those chance encounters when they happen.

### 7. Where to network

- **Casual conversations.** Talk to new people, just like that.
- **Career fairs, industry and professional networking events.** Keep an eye on CFG's communication channels for these.
- **LinkedIn.** The world's largest professional network, so have a presence on it. To reach seniors:
  1. Search for NUS alumni via the NUS LinkedIn page.
  2. Go to the page of a company you're interested in and search for NUS alumni working there.
  3. Start a conversation with them online, or reach out by email or phone.

### 8. Informational interviews

An **informal meeting** where _you_ interview someone to learn what it's really like to work in their role, company or industry, and what trends are coming. That way you can make an informed call on whether you want that path.

Several companies might advertise the same job title, but the actual work can be totally different. So talk to as many people as you can, within and across industries.

It's a golden opportunity to:

- ✅ Build a relationship with the connection
- ✅ Offer your own insights
- ✅ Find out if their company is right for you
- ✅ Potentially get referred for opportunities
- ✅ Get introduced to more connections

**How to ask:** message them on LinkedIn. A short note with your background and why you're reaching out lets them consider your request.

```
Hi [Name],

I'm [your name], from NUS [Faculty], [Year].
I noticed [your observation about the work they do].
I'm interested in [your area of interest / why you're connecting].
[Conversation starter question]

I look forward to hearing from you soon. Thank you.
```

### 9. Give back

Always give back to the network to grow and keep the connection. Don't just take and suck it dry.

**Within 48 hours of meeting**

- Thank-you email / LinkedIn message
- Thank-you post on social media

**Keep engaging**

- Schedule regular check-ins or catch-ups
- Connect on LinkedIn or other socials, with a personalised invite where possible
- Celebrate their accomplishments
- Comment on their posts or articles
- Tag them in your posts

**5-minute favours**

- Share an article or an interesting opportunity
- Make an introduction or referral
- Share your learnings or insights

## Final notes: how to do an internship well

> **BE FREAKING PROACTIVE.** Start conversations. Ask questions.

### Build clarity

- For any assignment, **restate it back** to your supervisor so they can correct you if you misunderstood.
- Clarify these early:
  - Who should I contact if I have questions?
  - What's the best way to communicate with you and the team (which app)?
  - Do I respond to emails & messages after work hours?
  - What are some helpful tasks I can do if I finish an assignment?

### Show competence

**Show your skill.** Before asking a question, make sure you've:

1. chosen the best person to ask
2. found a good time to ask
3. done your research, and can explain what you already know

**Show your motivation.**

- Speak up in meetings, or they'll think you're lazy and unmotivated.
- You come with a fresh perspective. Use it!
- Listen and pay attention.

### Build connections

- **Never eat alone.**
- Never say no to a lunch invitation.
- Be interested in other people.

Everything from the Networking section applies here too. You're already inside the company, so use it!

## Final Wrap up

### Career Alignment

Three parts. A career feels aligned when all three are in place:

|Component|What it is|Built through|
|---|---|---|
|**Identity**|Who you are: values, interests, strengths, RIASEC traits|Self-reflection, assessments|
|**Credibility**|Proof you can do it: skills, experience, track record|Internships, projects, CCAs, certifications|
|**Visibility**|What you show others: your personal brand|Resume, LinkedIn, networking|

**Missing one part creates a gap:**

|Missing|Gap|Looks like|
|---|---|---|
|Visibility|**Lost Opportunity**|Skilled and self-aware, but nobody knows (empty LinkedIn, generic resume)|
|Credibility|**Lost Trust**|Visible and clear on who they are, but can’t back it up|
|Identity|**Lost Purpose**|Capable and visible, but the work doesn’t fit who they are (“ticking off KPIs… but I wasn’t happy”)|

> There’s no point being the best at something if no one knows it. That’s Lost Opportunity.

**Career exploration starts with Identity.** Know your values, interests and strengths before you apply for internships or write your resume.

### Mindsets for career planning

**Challenge Mindset.** Ask _what problems do I want to work on?_, not _what job title do I want?_ One problem can lead to many roles and industries. This is step 2 of the elevator pitch.

**Design approach.** Treat your career like a prototype:

- Have a plan, but use it as a guide, not a script.
- Experiment, reflect on what worked and what didn’t, and iterate.
- ❌ A fixed 4-year plan, or chasing one dream job down every possible path.

**T-shaped professional.**

- **Horizontal bar = breadth**: transferable skills (communication, teamwork, problem-solving).
- **Vertical bar = depth**: real expertise in one domain.
- Not a generalist who knows a little of everything, and not a specialist with nothing else.

### Building your portfolio early

No internship yet? Start with:

- **Projects and competitions** to show hard skills applied.
- **CCAs and volunteering** to show soft skills and leadership.
- The **NUS Student Work Scheme** for real work experience on campus.
- ❌ Focusing _only_ on GPA. Grades alone don’t fill your portfolio.

**When you have several gaps**, don’t try to fix everything at once. Prioritise by:

1. Relevance to your target career.
2. What’s realistically achievable this academic year.
