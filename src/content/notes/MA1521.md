# Basics

$$
f_x = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \lim_{h\to0} \frac{f(x+h,y) - f(x, y)}{h}
$$

$x^2 - y^3 = 8xy$ this is the level curve of $f(x, y) = 0$
A **level curve** is a cruve where f equal some constant k at any point, usualy we say k = 0
the gradient of the $f$ - $\nabla f$ is perpendicular to the curve at any point. It holds wherever ∇f ≠ (0, 0). At points where the gradient is zero, like (0, 0) on your curve, it has no direction, so it can’t be perpendicular to anything.

$$
\triangle f(x, y) = f_x(a,b)(x-a) + f_y (a,b)(y-b) \approx f(x, y) - f(a, b)
$$

thus for the graph $z= f(x, y)$ the tangent plane of its surface at any point is

$$
z = f(a, b) + f_x\times(x-a) + f_y\times(y-b)
$$

this is the formula for the tangent plan in the graph

$f_x = 0$ and $f_y = 0$ these are the points where when u solve them simultaneously and get x,y you get the critical points.

**Clairaut's Theorum** - $f_{xy} = f_{yx}$ provided second derivative continuous

$$
D(a, b) = f_{xx}\ f_{yy} - (f_{xy})^2 = \begin{vmatrix} f_{xx} & f_{xy} \\ f_{yx} & f_{yy} \end{vmatrix}
$$

- $D > 0$ and $f_{xx} > 0,\ f_{yy} > 0$ - _local minimum_
- $D > 0$ and $f_{xx} < 0,\ f_{yy} < 0$ - _local maximum_
- $D < 0$ - saddle
- $D = 0$ inconclusive

> if D > 0, f_xx and f_yy _can’t_ have different signs. And if they do have different signs, D is automatically negative, so you have a saddle.

### Method of Least Squares

assume error function $y= ax + b \implies E(x,y) = ax + b - y$
given points: $(x_1, y_1),\ (x_2, y_2)\ ...$

$$
F(a, b) = \sum^{n}_{i=1} (E(x_i, y_i))^2 =  \sum^{n}_{i=1} (a\ x_i + b - y_i)^2
$$

find critical points of $F(a,b)$ ie get $F_a, F_b$
the variables that we are going to change and then get the a and b where its minimum.

same idea for higher order polynomials $P_n(x) = a_n\cdot x^n + a_{n-1}\cdot x^{n-1}+\cdot\cdot\cdot+a_0$

### Chain rule 1

$z = f(x, y)$ where $x = g(t)$ and $y=h(t)$
$$\therefore \frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\cdot \frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\cdot \frac{dy}{dt}$$

> Proof: $\delta f \approx \delta x \cdot f_x + \delta y \cdot f_y$ divide both sides by dt and you get the previous expression

### Chain rule 2

$z = f(x, y)$ where $x = g(u, v)$ and $y=h(u, v)$
$$\therefore \frac{\partial z}{\partial v} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\cdot \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\cdot \frac{\partial y}{\partial v}$$

### Directional Derivative

$\nabla f = \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} \right) = \left< f_x, f_y \right>$

the change in the function along a particular direction $\hat{u}$ is $\nabla f\cdot \hat{u}$ where $\hat{u}$ is unit vecotr

the direction of maximum increase we are trying to maximise $\nabla f\cdot \hat{u}$

$$
\nabla f\cdot \hat{u} = |\nabla f(x_o, y_o)||\hat{u}|\cos{\theta}\\
\text{to maximise cos must be 1} \\
\therefore \hat{u} = \frac{\nabla f(x_o, y_o)}{||\nabla f(x_o, y_o)||}
$$

in the plane $\nabla f$ gives the normal vector to the tangent plan at that point.

- For f(x, y), ∇f = ⟨f_x, f_y⟩ is a **2D** vector. It is normal to the **level curve** (tangent _line_) in the xy-plane, not to the 3D tangent plane of the graph.
- The normal to the tangent plane of the graph z = f(x, y) is **⟨f_x, f_y, −1⟩**.

L(x, y) = f(a, b ) + f_x(a, b)(x-a) + f_y(y-b)
$$

when $L(x,y) = z$ then thats the tangent plane

### Lagrange

∇f = λ∇g
That’s gradient of f equals λ times gradient of g, not f·u = g·λ. And λ isn’t really what you’re after. You’re after the **points (x, y)**. λ is a helper unknown that you solve for along the way and usually throw away.

**The method**

Solve these three equations for three unknowns (x, y, λ):

f_x = λg_x  
f_y = λg_y  
g(x, y) = c

Then plug every solution (x, y) into f. The largest value is the max and the smallest is the min. (This works when the constraint curve is closed and bounded, like a circle or ellipse. You also need ∇g ≠ 0 on the curve.)

**λ does have a meaning:** it’s how fast the optimal value of f changes as you loosen the constraint (increase c). In economics this is called the _shadow price_. You don’t need it for most problems, but it explains why it’s worth more than a throwaway variable
